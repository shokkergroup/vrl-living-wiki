01 · The goal

Give the archive a memory.

YouTube and podcast feeds remember upload dates. SHOKKER LORE remembers people, events, takes, jokes, victories, contradictions, callbacks, defining moments, and how all of them connect.

Preserve Define the canon and keep the official source reachable.
Understand Turn tape into receipts, entities, stories, and ledgers.
Experience Build native ways to search, play, explore, and participate.
Reuse Return research, clips, compilations, and context to the creator.
Compound Make every new upload enrich the entire connected archive.
Fan promise

Find anything. Play the proof.

A fan should be able to enter through an episode, person, topic, era, event, joke, ranking, question, or guided path and land on the exact source moment.

Creator promise

Your archive becomes a production desk.

The same memory powers prior-take lookup, episode prep, clip candidates, supercuts, resurfacing, corrections, and editorial review.

Business promise

Build once. Keep the story alive.

Productized setup creates the installed client. Automated monthly updates, feature modules, clip services, and new source lanes create recurring revenue.

Category: SHOKKER LORE is a creator memory world—not an AI wiki, transcript dump, generic podcast site, or standings tool.

02 · Universal foundation

What goes in every build.

These are the non-negotiable contracts. Their implementation can be reused. Their labels and presentation must still sound native to the client.

Core 01

Canon boundary

Explicit include, exclude, exception, continuation, and official-source rules.

Core 02

Source registry

Stable source ID, URL, date, duration, lane, captions, thumbnail, availability, fingerprint.

Core 03

Bounded receipts

Exact source, start, end, excerpt, evidence state, relation, and limitations.

Core 04

Entity registry

Canonical people, subjects, events, titles, teams, models, aliases, and collision quarantine.

Core 05

Source dossier

One page per canonical source with proof, recap, inside, connections, chronology, actions, and limits.

Core 06

Structured ledgers

Reviewed facts such as winners, titles, launches, rankings, verdicts, or claims that search must not guess.

Core 07

Ask + search router

Resolve identity and intent, answer from ledgers first, rank receipts second, use transcript last.

Core 08

Source playback

Official media, exact-second links, embed fallback, recovery route, and no mirrored-content dependency.

Core 09

Evidence ladder

Machine surfaced, human-curated candidate, editor verified, and creator certified remain distinct.

Core 10

Correction memory

Append-only corrections, preserved contradictions, and tombstones for removed sources.

Core 11

Fresh-tape intake

Discover → quarantine → review → promote. New uploads never bypass the declared evidence policy.

Core 12

Operations contract

Validation, analytics, accessibility, mobile behavior, export, backup, update cadence, and health checks.

Core 13

Indexable surface

Every canonical source gets a real, crawlable page with meaningful text in the served HTML — plus a sitemap, canonical URLs, and honest structured metadata.

Why indexability is a core contract

This is the one outcome a creator can measure without us. Their back catalog currently exists only inside a platform's search box; after launch, every episode is a durable public page that can be found, linked, and cited. Competing tools lead their marketing with exactly this — and it is the easiest promise to keep honestly, because it is a build decision, not a claim.

It also compounds with everything else in the core. A receipt is only durable if the page it lives on has a stable URL. A correction is only visible if the page can be re-crawled.

What ships, every time

  • a stable, human-readable URL per canonical source, entity, and major index;
  • meaningful text present in the served HTML — never rendered only after a script runs;
  • a generated sitemap covering every public route, regenerated on every build;
  • canonical URLs, and honest titles and descriptions written from the archive rather than a template;
  • structured metadata that matches what the page actually proves — never invented ratings, dates, or authorship;
  • an explicit no-index rule for review queues, internal tools, and unpromoted quarantine.
Ship the surface. Never promise rank, traffic, or timelines. The deliverable is a crawlable archive, and the report is a count of indexable pages — not a forecast.

04 · Trust engine

Colorful world. Precise claims.

Personality can be wild. Evidence language cannot be careless. Every public statement needs the right authority level.

Level 1

Machine surfaced

Candidate found by a rule, model, score, or metric. Useful for search and review queues; never automatic canon.

Level 2

Human curated

A person selected a structurally valid timestamp. It still does not authenticate the speaker or full context.

Level 3

Editor verified

An authenticated editor reviewed the source, surrounding context, and destination-lane policy.

Level 4

Creator certified

The owner confirms identity, intent, lore, or canonical label. Contradictory evidence remains visible.

Allowed inference language

  • “Available excerpts suggest…”
  • “Earliest known in the indexed archive…”
  • “The current evidence set contains…”
  • “This score is a discovery index, not an objective fact.”
  • “Co-present in one source; interaction is not established.”

Forbidden without stronger proof

  • “First ever.”
  • “They changed their mind here.”
  • “This commentary caused the incident.”
  • “The creator approved this.”
  • “This clip will go viral.”
  • Speaker names inferred from undiarized captions.

Start here

The four shapes

Across twenty-five genres, nearly every signature mechanic worth building turns out to be one of four shapes. Entering a new vertical, ask which one applies before inventing anything:

  • The Ledger — every claim, pick, or prediction, resolved against what actually happened. Called It dockets, prediction ledgers, theory trackers.
  • The Lineage — one thing traced across the whole archive over time. Bit bloodlines, song histories, car numbers, route evolution, an ordinance across years.
  • The Index — search by the thing people actually arrive looking for. The symptom, not the technique. The verse, not the sermon. The gear, not the trip.
  • The Path — a curated route that makes an intimidating archive enterable.
The Path is the watch-time engine's best friend — the only shape that converts a nervous newcomer into a binge. When in doubt in a new genre, build a Path first.

Pick two verticals. Own them.

The second build in a vertical is dramatically faster than the first — shared taxonomy, shared signature mechanics, shared clip signals, shared outreach language. Two verticals done deeply beats ten done once, and it produces referrals inside a community that already talks to itself.

Rank prospects on need and want separately. High need with low want is a trap: a hobbyist with 800 buried hours and no revenue needs this desperately and will never pay for it.

07 · Creator value

Turn memory into edit decisions.

The clip system should find defensible opportunities, explain why they surfaced, and hand an editor a useful starting point. It does not pretend to finish judgment.

Candidate

Short moment

One self-contained source window with a clear hook, payoff, context risk, and proposed runtime.

Package

Supercut

At least three receipts across at least two sources, built around a defensible theme rather than a false “origin.”

Resurface

Old + new

An earlier and later source connected by a real subject, with no automatic claim that a prediction came true or a mind changed.

Every candidate carries Never inferred Human gate
Source ID, URL, timestamp, proposed in/out, bounded excerpt. Speaker from auto-captions alone. Watch surrounding context and identify speakers.
Channel-specific component score and explanation. Guaranteed virality or business result. Approve editorial value and current relevance.
Context, language, allegation, rights, and staleness risk. Rights clearance or platform approval. Check rights, sponsors, language, and policy.
Suggested title, hook, caption, and treatment labeled new copy. That promotional copy is an archival quote. Set final wording, framing, graphics, and export.
Learn from editor choices, final edit decisions, and post-performance metrics. Never let taste or performance rewrite source receipts, evidence, canon, or historical scores.

08 · Problems already paid for

Do not relearn these lessons.

VRL and WWAM exposed the failure modes that appear when an archive becomes ambitious. These rules belong in every future build.

Trap What happened Permanent solution
Source count ≠ event count VRL continuation broadcasts initially appeared as separate races. Model canonical events separately from source uploads; preserve source role and continuation provenance.
Titles are not scope proof Uploads can look relevant while belonging to mock races, technical fragments, weekends, or unrelated lanes. Make eligibility executable. Partition every candidate exactly once and keep the exclusion report.
Finale winner ≠ champion A tempting inference turned race winners into season champions. Use an independently reviewed championship/result ledger. Unknown remains unknown.
Mention ≠ participation VRL driver indexes mixed actual participants with names merely mentioned by the booth. Separate mentions, appearances, starts, results, and official entries. Label mixed indexes honestly.
Alias collision Names such as surnames or nicknames could silently resolve to the wrong person. Give aliases explicit ownership, quarantine collisions, and refuse ambiguous Ask answers.
Keyword search beats truth Generic name matches could outrank the direct result/date answer. Resolve entity and intent; answer from the most authoritative structured ledger first.
Incident bias “Wildest” rankings over-rewarded wrecks, profanity, and disruption. Use visible multi-part formulas, cap sensational signals, add diversity rules, and show evidence confidence.
Phrase match ≠ visual proof A phrase such as “upside down” created rollover candidates without visual verification. Label them review candidates and keep visual claims behind a human gate.
Chronology ≠ causality Announcer’s Curse-style pairs could imply commentary caused later action. Publish thresholds, chronology, confidence, and an explicit no-causality rule.
Registry breadth ≠ evidence depth WWAM had a broad canonical registry, a smaller promoted set, and a smaller memory-ready set. Report registry, caption, promoted, memory-ready, and gap counts independently.
Related source ≠ subject evidence A globally relevant receipt could leak into an exact-source or subject-specific answer. Bind exact-source queries to the requested source fingerprint and require an evidence relationship to the requested subject.
Character mention ≠ performance Unbounded mentions could masquerade as recurring-character performances. Maintain separate machine-echo and bounded performance lanes.
Lineage ≠ origin Ordered recurrences tempt “first ever,” intentional callback, or influence claims. Call it earliest known in the eligible index and preserve the lineage definition.
Generated voice ≠ archive quote Fan-made character riffs can sit beside proof but must never become proof. Visually separate synthesis, promotional copy, fan prose, and archival excerpts.
Local taste ≠ creator approval A person clicking A/B choices is not authenticated as the creator. Label local operators, bind preference artifacts, cap modifiers, and protect evidence fields.
Checksum ≠ authentication Deterministic fingerprints detect drift but do not prove identity or authority. Describe fingerprints as consistency checks; use real authentication for production decisions.
Future-text leakage A synchronized companion can spoil upcoming moments if labels are exposed early. Expose full event text only after the timestamp is crossed; future markers may show time remaining only.
Co-presence ≠ interaction Two entities in one source do not prove they spoke, interacted, agreed, or influenced each other. Count source coverage separately from relationships and label same-source intersections narrowly.
Feature sprawl A powerful demo can accumulate too many equally loud doors. Choose three primary front doors, progressively disclose depth, and keep creator proof separate from the fan opening.
New uploads corrupt canon A fresh source can silently enter public counts before its lane and identity are reviewed. Quarantine first. Promote only after scope, source, and evidence checks pass.

08B · The HLRN correction case

A bad first build became the permanent build standard.

HLRN is the clearest end-to-end example of why copying a successful site’s navigation is not the same as reproducing its depth. The first HLRN release had the right nouns and the wrong public data contract. The corrective release rebuilt the editorial layer, visual dossiers, race paper, interactions, creator workflow, and deployment verification from evidence outward.

What the owner actually asked for

Same depth. Different world.

  • Document official HLRN Seasons 1 and 2 from one livestream channel.
  • Keep non-league races in a separate bonus lane called Highline Live.
  • Carry VRL’s Watch, Ask, Highlights, Scene, Drivers, Seasons, Rankings, and Explore contract.
  • Rename Scene to Highline Central and make it a real newspaper-style publication.
  • Give HLRN a visual and interaction language that cannot be mistaken for VRL.
  • Build honestly without complete owner result sheets, then accept those sheets later.
Canon before interface

Three public source lanes

  1. Official HLRN: 20 canonical race files—16 in Season 1 and 4 in Season 2.
  2. Highline Live: 29 non-league races, specials, memorials, practices, and one-offs.
  3. Fragments: 3 partial or limited sources retained in the ledger without becoming official races.

Forty auxiliary and companion sources can support context or entertainment but never silently enter championship math.

The first-build failure, stated plainly: transcript proximity was serialized as public editorial. A generic title function produced 161 “Incident signal on the scanner” cards, 25 “Trevor Haley in the incident call” cards, and 21 “Closing signal: Trevor Haley at the white flag” cards. Those cards contaminated Highlights, race pages, rankings, and driver dossiers. The interface was technically populated and editorially broken.

GitHub assets were silently clipped A connector transport truncated large base64 outputs at roughly 30,000 characters even though blob creation returned success. Read source files in 19,998-byte chunks, concatenated base64 on 3-byte boundaries, rebuilt blobs, and compared all deployed bytes to local files. HTTP 200 is insufficient. Release requires byte-for-byte verification of every published asset and a live-origin browser suite.
Local success was treated as launch proof Earlier checks did not prove cache propagation, real subpath behavior, or public asset integrity. Ran desktop/mobile interaction QA against the GitHub Pages origin after cache-busting asset checks. Local QA, remote byte audit, public content contract, and public browser QA are four separate gates.

The recovery sequence

Repair the contract before polishing the screen.

Re-audit the reference

Inspect VRL route by route and name the anatomy of Scene, driver dossiers, race deep dives, Ask, playback, results, and trust surfaces.

Freeze the contaminated layer

Count repeated titles and affected consumers. Keep the bad candidates for research, but remove their path into public cards.

Author the official season spine

Write one structured editorial object per official race: cover line, headline, deck, lead, beats, notebook, After Hours, and limits.

Rebuild all consumers

Generate Highlights, Central, drivers, rankings, and race pages from the reviewed ledger instead of patching pages individually.

Extract visual receipts

Export frames from HLRN-owned tape at reviewed timestamps, store their provenance, and keep honest fallbacks for unmapped identities.

Add HLRN-native participation

Build Race Night Mixer, Battle Lines, Compare, Replay, Studio, and Pulse around HLRN’s companion-show and high-line vocabulary.

Validate the public contract

Assert exact counts, uniqueness, review states, source resolution, image loading, persistence, export workflows, forms, responsive layout, and console silence.

Verify deployment as data

Compare bytes, wait for Pages propagation, cache-bust assets, rerun interaction QA on the public origin, and only then call the release live.

Token-efficient production

Use models for judgment. Use code for repetition.

Work Efficient path Wasteful path to avoid
Inventory Fetch metadata once; partition lanes with deterministic rules; save stable JSON. Re-reading channel pages or re-describing every upload in conversation.

Transport lesson: when base64 must cross a tool output boundary, choose a raw byte chunk divisible by three. HLRN used 19,998-byte chunks, producing 26,664 base64 characters per call—safely below the observed clipping ceiling. Concatenate those base64 chunks in order, create the blob, then verify the remote bytes. Do not split base64 at arbitrary padded boundaries.

09 · Token-efficient production

Spend intelligence where judgment matters.

Metadata, filtering, parsing, IDs, exact timestamps, validation, deduplication, and rebuilds belong in deterministic code. Use model reasoning for interpretation, synthesis, creative direction, and genuinely ambiguous evidence.

Before models

Inventory and prune

Fetch official metadata, deduplicate, apply scope rules, group continuations, measure captions, and skip excluded tape before any deep analysis.

During models

Distill once

Chunk with stable boundaries, request structured output, retain timestamps, cache every result, and synthesize from dossiers rather than raw transcripts.

After models

Rebuild deltas

Fingerprint inputs, process only changed/new sources, recompute affected graphs and rankings, and test against frozen fixtures.

Efficient hierarchy

  1. Raw cache: captions, metadata, source files.
  2. Source distill: recap, entities, moments, quotes, claims, limitations.
  3. Canonical dossier: normalized source record and evidence graph edges.
  4. Cross-source derivations: timelines, rankings, lineages, comparisons, resurfacing.
  5. Presentation: render from stable artifacts; never ask a model to rediscover the archive at page load.

Token waste to eliminate

  • Re-reading whole transcripts for every feature.
  • Sending excluded or duplicate sources to a model.
  • Asking a model to generate IDs, sort deterministically, or validate timestamps.
  • Re-summarizing an unchanged source after every new upload.
  • Stuffing the whole corpus into Ask instead of routing through ledgers and indexes.
  • Using large prompts when one schema and one reference pack would do.
  • Running expensive reasoning on obvious machine candidates before triage.

09b · The real economics

Capacity, not cost.

Production runs on a flat monthly subscription with a weekly usage limit — not metered per-token billing. That single fact changes what the estimator above is measuring. Tokens are not a bill. They are a budget of weekly capacity, and the only question that matters is how many builds fit inside a week.

Measured, not estimated

Two live proof worlds, built on the plan:

  • VRL — 222 source broadcasts transcribed and distilled, 219 canonical events, 486 hours of tape, 185 reconciled identities. Distill cost: ~100–115k tokens per race, hard-capped batches, 3 agents max concurrency.
  • WWAM — 510 canonical sources, of which only 111 are caption-backed (390 metadata-only, 9 caption-limited). 1,490 registered receipts, 928 source-bound artifacts, 29 reusable engines, 14 interfaces, 112 regression files — the bulk of it inside a 24-hour window.

Both archives, plus every research pass and the entire engine buildout, consumed under half of one week's limit.

What that implies

  • Roughly 330 distilled sources plus full product buildout fit in half a week.
  • A week comfortably absorbs two deep archives and their assembly — and distillation alone is only a fraction of that half-week, because most of it went to engines and interfaces that are now reusable.
  • Marginal model cost of one additional client build is, in practice, zero.
  • Spread a flat monthly plan across four builds and the fully loaded cost is a low-double-digit figure per build — against quotes in the hundreds to low thousands.
The binding constraint is operator active hours, then wall-clock agent time, then weekly limit — in that order. Weekly limit is not close to binding at a sane build cadence.

Why the archive band still exists

It is not a token-cost pass-through. A bigger archive costs more in the things that are scarce:

  • Review time. More sources means more alias collisions, more identity calls, more gate failures to triage — all human.
  • Canon decisions. More eras, more broadcaster changes, more exception rules to author and defend.
  • Wall-clock. Deep archives occupy the unattended pipeline for longer, which is what actually limits builds-per-week.
  • Delivered value. A ten-year archive is worth more to its owner than a single season. Price follows value, not cost.

Quote the band honestly on those grounds. Do not tell a client it reflects compute — it does not, and the claim will not survive a curious question.

The lane that costs almost nothing

WWAM shipped 390 metadata-only pages beside 111 distilled ones — with honest zero-states, real artwork, working players, and no invented claims. That is a legitimate, shippable product state, not a shortfall.

It means archive size and archive spend are decoupled. Band a client on caption-backed sources, not total uploads. A 3,000-upload channel with 400 caption-backed broadcasts is a mid-band build wearing a large number.

Sell the honest split: everything catalogued and playable, caption-backed tape distilled deeply, coverage published in plain numbers.

Streamline the distill pass

~100–115k tokens per source is the current measured baseline. These are the levers that move it, roughly in order of impact:

  1. Cache the invariant prefix. The Channel Pack, output schema, vocabulary, and reference examples are byte-identical across every source in a project. Put them first, behind one cache breakpoint, and let the per-source transcript be the only thing that varies. This is the single biggest reduction available and it is currently the most commonly missed.
  2. Lower effort on bulk passes. Distillation is structured extraction, not deep reasoning. Reserve high effort for judgment work — evidence calls, canon boundaries, ranking synthesis, art direction.
  3. Demand structured output, not prose. A tight schema cuts output tokens hard, and output is the expensive half.
  4. Route metadata-only sources around the model entirely. No captions means no distill call — build the honest page deterministically.
  5. Never re-read a transcript. Every later feature reads the cached distill, never the raw tape. One pass per source, ever.
  6. Fingerprint and rebuild deltas. An unchanged source is never re-distilled, no matter how many features are added downstream.
  7. Keep the deterministic lane deterministic. Fetch, eligibility, transcripts, normalization, clustering, mentions, keyword radar, ranking math, frame grabs, and validation are all local code with zero model spend. Every task that migrates from the model to the pipeline is a permanent capacity win.

Do not chase the wrong savings

  • Do not disable reasoning outright to save tokens on the current generation — it degrades tool reliability in ways that cost more time than it saves. Lower the effort level instead.
  • Do not shrink the reference pack. Cached prefix tokens are nearly free; a thinner pack produces worse distills and more review time, which is the expensive resource.
  • Do not batch unrelated sources into one call to save overhead. Cross-contamination between sources is a canon bug, and canon bugs cost human hours.
  • Do not optimize before measuring. Log tokens per source per project. If a project drifts far above the ~115k baseline, the cause is almost always an uncached prefix or a re-read transcript — find it rather than trimming prompts.
Optimize for operator hours per launch. Token efficiency only matters where it buys back attention or wall-clock.

16 · The revenue system

The archive should earn again.

Every competing tool sells organization. This sells an archive that pays. Embedded playback counts toward the creator's public watch hours, ads run inside those embeds, and every cent of that revenue goes to the channel owner — the embedding site earns nothing. That last fact is a weapon: we can say we take no cut, and mean it literally.

Verified

Embeds count

Watch time from videos embedded on external sites counts toward public watch hours, including the 4,000-hour monetization threshold.

Verified

Ads run

Pre-roll, mid-roll, and banner ads serve inside embedded players — and ad frequency on embeds increased in August 2024.

Verified

Creator keeps 100%

Only YouTube and the video owner earn. The embedding site earns nothing. We never mirror media, so we never take revenue away.

The constraint

Intent is required

A view needs a deliberate Play click and roughly 30 seconds. Autoplay — muted or hidden especially — does not count and violates policy.

Governing principle: manufacture intent, never manufacture plays. A fan who reads what happens in a moment and clicks to watch it is the most intent-driven viewer on the internet. We are not gaming the system — we produce exactly the behavior it is built to reward.

The 30-second floor · a hard build rule

Because a view needs roughly thirty seconds, the receipt compiler must never emit a play door that cannot reach it:

  • never start a clip within 30 seconds of the end of a source;
  • start slightly before the payoff, so the viewer has a reason to stay through the threshold instead of getting the punchline at second three and leaving;
  • enforce it as a gate — a moment that cannot clear the floor is a text citation, not a play door.

This is the highest-leverage detail in the system and it is invisible to anyone who has not thought about it.

Page weight is a watch-time lever

Time-to-interactive determines whether a visitor is still present to click Play. A slow page loses the play before the thirty-second clock ever starts.

Compress art, ship responsive image sizes, lazy-load below the fold, split bundles per surface, and load thumbnails from YouTube's own image CDN rather than copying them into the build. A multi-megabyte logo is not a hosting problem — it is lost revenue for the client.

Convergence

The moment ledger is Clip markup

Google's Clip structured data marks named, bounded segments of a video so Search can show Key Moments — jump-to-second links beneath the result. SeekToAction tells Google the player supports URL-based seeking.

Our moment ledger already carries exactly what Clip requires: a name, an exact start, an exact end, bound to one canonical source. The mapping is one-to-one. Emit it on every caption-backed source page and the creator's videos can surface in Google with jump-to-moment links — each one a deliberate click from Search straight into their video at a specific second.

Nobody else in the category can do this, because nobody else has curated bounded moments. This is where the indexable-surface contract (Core 13) and the revenue system become one machine.

Compliance spine · absolute rules

  • No autoplay. Ever. Not muted, not hidden, not “just the first one.”
  • No hidden, off-screen, zero-size, or background players.
  • Never more than one player active at a time.
  • No auto-advance — the next moment requires a click.
  • No incentivized viewing, points for plays, or watch-to-unlock.
  • No bots, scheduled playback, or self-refreshing pages.
  • Only public sources enter the play surface.
  • Never link to or promote any view-count or subscriber service.
  • Never promise views, hours, revenue, or rank in a proposal.
A policy strike on a client channel would end the business. No amount of watch time is worth it. Publish a plain “how this archive works” page on every client site stating that we never autoplay, never mirror media, and always send playback to the official channel.

Why the rules are also better product

  • Autoplay produces uncounted plays and annoyed visitors; clicked plays count and convert.
  • One player at a time means the moment gets full attention — which is what carries someone past thirty seconds.
  • Refusing to gamify playback keeps the experience about the lore, which is the only reason anyone returns.

Sales language

“Your back catalog stops being a graveyard and starts being a second channel.”

Then: their product makes the archive searchable, ours makes it pay; we never copy the media, so every play is on their channel with their ads and we earn nothing from it; and every month they get a report plus instructions for verifying it against YouTube's own analytics.
